Finding the thread.

Building a portfolio that connects the work, the person, and the choices behind both.

In this note

A career needs a connecting idea.

My work has moved through banking mainframes, cloud engineering, and applied AI. Putting those roles in date order tells you where I have been. I wanted the site to explain what connects them.

I make complex technology useful.

That sentence became the starting point. The thread follows the same habit through different systems: understand what is happening, find the part that needs attention, and make the result useful to the people relying on it.

It also gave the page a structure. The introduction explains how I think. The backstory shows how that developed. The selected stories give concrete examples of it in practice.

Beige and brown felt like me.

The colour direction started with a simple preference: I liked beige and brown. Cream gives the page room to breathe; dark brown keeps the writing clear; a warmer brown picks out links and details.

The portrait needed more care. A subject cut out against a flat background felt pasted on. Soft light and shadows helped the person and the space belong together.

We tried changing the T-shirt to white. I preferred the original black shirt, with the logo removed. Keeping my face intact mattered more than producing an idealised studio portrait.

One word carries the emphasis.

I explored more expressive headline fonts, including tall, high-contrast serifs. They were interesting in isolation, but the result felt disconnected from the rest of the site. I returned to the original editorial direction.

I make complex
technology useful.Outfit for the voice. An italic accent for the point.

The italic on “useful” is a small decision with a clear purpose. It puts emphasis on the outcome. Monospaced labels add a connection to engineering without making every paragraph look like a terminal.

A small signature: fp.

The mark uses my initials, set in lowercase with a brown full stop. In the header, it sits beside my full name: a compact signature with a clear introduction.

The header mark uses Outfit, the same typeface as the rest of the interface. Its heavier weight and tight letter spacing bring the two letters together into a recognisable shape. The lowercase forms keep it informal, which fits the personal tone of the page.

The full stop gives the mark an ending and introduces the warm brown accent in a very small area. It does not need to explain mainframes, cloud, or AI; those ideas have their own space in the writing and the thread. The mark’s job is to identify whose site you are visiting.

The header implements it as live text, so it stays sharp as the layout changes and doubles as the home link. The browser-tab favicon repeats the initials and accent on a rounded cream tile. Its letterforms are outlined from the same Outfit weight and spacing as the header, so the favicon keeps its shape without relying on a font being installed or loaded.

Give movement something to explain.

The Git-style career timeline was an early idea I wanted to keep. Its connected paths give the progression a shape, and revealing it as you scroll supports the act of reading through the backstory.

The thread brings systems, cloud, and applied AI into the same visual space. Those words give the abstract form context. Without them, some of its personality was lost.

There were limits. Flipping forms needed a stronger relationship to the pointer. Bigger typography and more motion did not automatically make the page feel better. The small keyboard in the footer became another exercise in restraint: compact enough to belong there, with enough depth to still read as keys.

Give each part of the page a different job.

The opening is a portrait and a proposition. The photograph places me on the right, leaving space for the headline on the left. That empty space is part of the composition: it makes the sentence readable without putting it in a separate box or covering my face.

The background and the page share a warm colour family, so the portrait feels connected to its surroundings. The studio treatment is built from two image layers inside an SVG. A feathered mask preserves the face from the reference image over the softened background. This keeps the accepted portrait while allowing a different setting around it.

Further down, the thread and the personal introduction sit alongside each other on larger screens. This shortens the page and lets the visual respond to the passage being read. On a narrow screen, they stack so the text retains a comfortable width.

The backstory and the selected stories answer different questions. The timeline explains the progression of my responsibilities. The stories go deeper into particular problems and decisions. Separating those roles helps avoid repeating the same career summary in every section.

Rounded corners soften the larger image and story panels. Most writing sits directly on the page, with spacing doing the grouping. The keyboard gets more physical depth because its shape communicates an interaction: these are things you can press.

The stack follows the page.

The selected Editorial version is a static website built with semantic HTML, native CSS, and plain JavaScript. It does not run on React or Next.js. There is no application framework or build step required to serve this version.

HTML

Sections, headings, links, and expandable story content carry the information. The career and project writing lives in the document rather than being drawn into the animated canvas.

CSS

Custom properties define the palette. Grid and Flexbox arrange the sections, media queries adapt the composition, and gradients, borders, and shadows give the footer keys their depth. Their press response comes from CSS transforms.

Canvas 2D

The thread sculpture is custom JavaScript. It calculates points in three dimensions, rotates them, projects them onto a flat canvas, and draws the connecting strands in depth order. The appearance is spatial, while the rendering uses the browser’s 2D drawing API.

SVG

The career graph uses vector paths measured against the text. SVG also supplies the architecture illustrations, portrait masking, and footer linework. Those elements stay sharp as their containers change size.

Local fonts and assets

Outfit and IBM Plex Mono are served with the site. The fonts use font-display: swap, so text can appear while the font files load. Portrait assets are local too.

For this version, those browser primitives cover the interactions we need. The site can be previewed with a simple local HTTP server and hosted as static files. A publishing system may become useful as Notes grows; a single first article does not require one.

Choosing how to build it.

Most of the site’s structure comes from HTML and CSS. The more expressive parts use two drawing techniques: Canvas for the moving thread, and SVG for the career timeline and architecture diagrams.

Each suits a different job. The thread redraws as you interact with it; the timeline needs precise connections to the surrounding text. The contact keys remain ordinary links, with CSS providing their shape, depth, and pressed state.

This keeps the implementation close to the experience: familiar browser behaviour for navigation and reading, with custom rendering where it helps tell the story.

The small engineering decisions matter.

The thread runs through requestAnimationFrame. Pointer movement changes its orientation; dragging adds momentum that decays after release. A capped speed boost makes a quick gesture feel different from a slow one. The loop pauses when the sculpture is outside the viewport or the document is hidden.

Its drawing resolution follows the display’s pixel density, capped at two. That is a trade-off between crisp lines and the amount of work done each frame. The drawing also sorts the strands by depth, which helps communicate volume without loading a 3D engine.

The timeline needed a different solution. Hard-coded node positions drifted away from the text as fonts and layout changed. The current graph measures its anchors after fonts load and recalculates when its container resizes. The muted path and the revealed path share the same geometry; a clipping rectangle controls how much of the active path is visible as you scroll in either direction.

Motion has controls. The sculpture has a pause button, the chapter tabs support keyboard navigation, and reduced-motion preferences change the animated behaviour. These are implementation choices rather than proof of a complete accessibility audit; they make the experience more controllable while keeping the written story available.

Responsive testing matters beyond stacking columns. A mobile selector intended for the header also hid the Email key because both areas used a navigation element. Scoping that rule to the header fixed the problem at its source. It is a useful reminder that shared styles need boundaries as a page grows.

Building with AI still means making decisions.

I built this site iteratively with Codex. References helped describe a direction; local versions made it possible to judge the result in the browser. The useful part of the process was being able to try an idea, react to it, and refine it.

My feedback was often specific: preserve the face, soften the shadows, restore the timeline, reduce the size of the contact keys. Sometimes the right next step was to revert a change.

The same approach applied to implementation. A convincing picture of an interaction is only a starting point. The actual page has to respond to input, preserve readable text, and hold together when the viewport changes. Looking at the browser repeatedly was part of the design process.

There is still technical housekeeping to do. The stylesheet contains successive overrides from the experiments, and some alternate pages remain alongside the chosen version. Consolidating those rules and removing obsolete variants would be a sensible preparation for publishing. I would also measure performance on the deployed site before making claims about its speed.

The last part was editing.

The strongest direction emerged through decisions about what to keep: the warm palette, the original portrait, the thread, the Git history, and a single italic word.

Making something personal involves more than choosing a visual reference. It means noticing when an attractive idea does not fit, and being willing to remove it. That turned out to be as much a part of building this portfolio as adding anything new.
